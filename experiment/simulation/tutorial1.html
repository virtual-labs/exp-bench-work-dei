Measurement, Measuring Tools & Layout Tools

1. Introduction

Accurate interpretation and measurement of dimensions are skills that every individual involved in a mechanical shop must have. A thorough understanding of units of measure, measurement systems, and measurement terms is necessary for proficiency in workshop technology. This chapter presents information on the basics of measurement, measuring tools, and layout tools used in mechanical shops. It outlines the basic knowledge (principles and practice) required in using the most common measuring instruments.

Historical Context: Before the Industrial Revolution, the craftsman used to make and carry his own tools and measuring gadgets. The manufacture of products was totally dependent on the skill of the craftsman and the accuracy of his tools. As a consequence, no two identical parts were interchangeable, and standards could not be met in production.

Once the Industrial Revolution took place, the need arose for the division of labor, simplification, and standardization to enable mass production. As parts were produced in large quantities according to specifications, interchangeability became the norm of production. Interchangeability is the ability of parts to be substituted for one another without the need for part adjustment during assembly.

2. Need of Measurement

When we talk of the length of an object, we mean the distance from one end to the other expressed numerically in convenient units. In order to find the length of a certain object, we measure by comparing the object to some other object of standard length that is internationally accepted.

Without measurements, the quality of products cannot be assessed; therefore, every manufactured product must be measured in some way. Since all manufacturing processes have some inherent variability, manufactured parts must be checked numerically to determine whether they meet design specifications (size, volume, density, pressure, heat, impedance, brightness, etc.), including tolerances and surface finish.

3. What is Measurement?

The determination of a dimension of a part through the use of some type of device that permits the magnitude of the dimension to be determined directly or indirectly in terms of scalar units is known as measurement.

It is the result of quantitative comparison between a predefined standard and the unknown quantity. There are two basic requirements for measurements:

  1. The standard should be accurately known and internationally accepted.
  2. The device and experimental procedure adopted for comparison must be provable.

4. Classification of Measurement

Figure 1: Classification of Measurement Techniques

A. According to type of measurement

  • Linear measurement: Measurement of distance or length (length, width, height). The basic metric unit is the meter (m). Instruments: Steel rule, Vernier caliper, Micrometer.
  • Angular measurement: Measurement of angles. Instruments typically have circular division scales, e.g., Protractor.
  • Comparative measurement: Deals with relative measurements such as flatness, straightness, and surface finish. Instruments: Non-graduated tools like fixed gauges, optical flats.

B. According to the nature of measurement

  • Direct measurement: Value obtained directly (e.g., scale, vernier caliper).
  • Indirect measurement: Dimension determined by measuring other values functionally related to the required value (e.g., sine bar, calipers).

C. According to contact with the workpiece

  • Contact measurement: Tip of the instrument touches the surface (e.g., Micrometer, dial indicator).
  • Contactless measurement: No contact required (e.g., Tool maker's microscope, projection comparator).

D. According to the type of Inspection

  • Online Inspection: Part inspection during processing.
  • Offline Inspection: Inspection of parts after they are produced.

5. Systems of Measurement

English System (Imperial)

The common unit is the inch (in). It can be divided into fractional divisions (e.g., 1/2 inch) or decimal divisions (e.g., 0.500 inch). Using the fractional method, 1/64 inch is the smallest fractional size accurately measureable by eye.

Metric System (SI)

The metric system is based on a decimal system (powers of ten). It simplifies calculations by using a set of prefixes.

6. Important Terms in Metrology

Metrology The science of measurements such as length, thickness, diameter, angle, flatness, straightness, roundness, profiles, and surface roughness.
Calibration Determining by how much the instrument reading is in error by checking it against a measurement standard of known error. It gives information about the error relative to an accepted reference value.
Error The indication of a measuring instrument minus the "true" value. Error is not a mistake; it is a scientific variance.
Accuracy (Correctness) The closeness of agreement between a test result and the accepted reference value.
Precision (Repeatability) The agreement of a set of measures taken in the same manner. It refers to the degree of exactness or repeatability.
Resolution (Sensitivity) The smallest difference in dimensions that an instrument can detect (the smallest marked increment).
Tolerance The amount of variation in basic dimension that is tolerated without impairing the functional fitness of the design part.
Traceability Ensuring a measured result can be related to national or international standards through an unbroken chain of comparisons.

7. Control of Measurement Conditions

In every measurement, four primary conditions must be controlled:

  1. Control of the Part: Requires manufacturing controls to produce precisely machined surfaces.
  2. Built-in Design Features: Features permitting accurate readings to be taken consistently.
  3. The Engineer: Expertise in taking and reading a measurement.
  4. Environment: Precision measurements require adequate temperature controls. Vibration, dust, and other environmental conditions must be eliminated.
